p031 (429–452): Hecate gives powerful help to anyone she chooses.
In the public assembly, she makes whoever she favors stand out.
And when men arm themselves for war that destroys lives,
the goddess stands beside those she chooses,
ready to grant them victory and glory.
She sits beside respected kings as they give judgment.

She helps, too, when men compete in games:
the goddess stands beside them and lends her strength.
The winner, victorious through power and strength,
easily carries off a fine prize, full of joy,
and brings glory to his parents.
She is good at helping riders, too, whenever she chooses.

For those who work the gray, rough sea
and pray to Hecate and the thundering Earth-shaker,
the glorious goddess easily grants a great catch—
and easily takes it away when it is in sight,
if that is what she wants.

With Hermes, she is good at making livestock thrive in their pens.
Herds of cattle, great herds of goats,
and flocks of woolly sheep—if she wishes,
she makes a few grow into many, or many dwindle to a few.

So, though her mother had no other child,
Hecate holds honored privileges among all the immortal gods.
Zeus, the son of Cronus, made her the nurse of all the young
who, after her, opened their eyes to the light of far-seeing Dawn.
From the beginning she has cared for the young.
These are her honors.

p032 (453–467): Rhea had glorious children with Cronus:
Hestia, Demeter, and Hera with her golden sandals;
mighty Hades, whose heart knows no pity,
who lives in halls beneath the earth;
the thundering Earth-shaker, Poseidon;
and Zeus, wise in planning, father of gods and humans,
whose thunder makes the wide earth shake.

Great Cronus swallowed his children, each one
as it came from its mother's sacred womb to her knees.
He meant to keep any other glorious descendant of Uranus
from holding the royal honor among the immortal gods.
For he had learned from Gaia and starry Uranus
that he was destined, strong as he was,
to be overcome by his own son,
through the plans of great Zeus.

So Cronus kept watch with his eyes wide open,
and swallowed his own children as he waited for them.
Rhea was gripped by grief that would not fade.

p033 (468–484): But when Rhea was about to give birth to Zeus,
father of gods and humans,
she begged her own dear parents,
Gaia and starry Uranus, to help her devise a plan:
to give birth to her dear child in secret,
and to win revenge for her father
and for the children great Cronus, with his crooked schemes,
had swallowed.

They listened closely to their dear daughter and agreed to help.
They told her all that fate had in store
for King Cronus and his strong-hearted son.
They sent her to Lyctus, in the fertile land of Crete,
when she was about to bear her youngest child,
great Zeus. Vast Gaia received him from her
to nourish and care for him on the broad island of Crete.

Through the swift, black night Rhea carried him,
coming first to Lyctus.
Taking him in her arms, she hid him
in a deep cave, in the hidden places of the holy earth,
on Mount Aegeum, thick with forest.
