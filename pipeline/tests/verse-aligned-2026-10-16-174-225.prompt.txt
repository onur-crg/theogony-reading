p014 (174–187): Gaia, the goddess Earth, hid her son Cronus in ambush.
She put a jagged-toothed sickle in his hands
and told him the whole plan.

Then mighty Uranus, the god Sky, came bringing night.
Longing to make love, he spread himself
all over Earth. From his hiding place,
his son reached out with his left hand;
with his right he gripped the huge, long, jagged sickle
and swiftly sliced off his own father’s genitals,
flinging them behind him.

But they did not leave his hand without a result.
Earth caught every drop of blood that scattered,
and as the years rolled round, she gave birth
to the powerful Erinyes, spirits of vengeance,
and the great Giants, gleaming in armor,
long spears in their hands,
and the Meliae, ash-tree spirits,
whom people across the boundless earth call by that name.

p015 (188–206): Once Cronus had cut off the genitals
with adamant—a substance nothing could break—
and thrown them from the land into the surging sea,
they drifted over the water for a long time.
White foam rose around the immortal flesh,
and in that foam a girl grew.

First she drew near holy Cythera, an island;
then she reached Cyprus, surrounded by the sea.
Out stepped a beautiful goddess, worthy of reverence,
and grass grew beneath her slender feet.

Gods and humans call her Aphrodite, the goddess of love,
because she grew in foam—the Greek word is aphros.
They call her Cytherea because she came to Cythera,
and Cyprus-born because she was born on wave-washed Cyprus,
and “Genital-loving” because she appeared from genitals.

Eros, the god Love, went with her,
and beautiful Himeros, the god Desire, followed,
from the moment of her birth
as she went to join the company of gods.

From the beginning, this has been her honor,
the share assigned to her among humans and immortal gods:
young women’s intimate whispers, smiles and deceptions,
sweet pleasure, lovemaking, and tenderness.

p016 (207–210): Their father, mighty Sky, called the children
he himself had fathered “Titans,” in reproach.
He said they had strained too far in their recklessness,
committing a terrible deed—
and that punishment for it would come later.

p017 (211–225): Night gave birth to hateful Moros, the doom awaiting us,
to black Ker, a spirit of death, and to Thanatos, Death himself.
She bore Hypnos, Sleep, and the whole tribe of Dreams.
The dark goddess Night bore them without sleeping with anyone.

Then she bore Momus, Blame, and Oizys, painful Misery,
and the Hesperides, maidens who tend the beautiful golden apples
and the trees that bear them,
beyond famous Oceanus, the river circling the world.

She also bore the Fates and the Keres, merciless avengers—
Clotho, Lachesis, and Atropos,
who give mortals their share of good and bad at birth.
They pursue the wrongdoing of humans and gods,
and these goddesses never let their terrible anger rest
until they make whoever has done wrong pay dearly.

Deadly Night also bore Nemesis, the goddess of payback,
a source of suffering for mortals.
After her she bore Apate, Deceit, and Philotes, Intimacy,
and destructive Old Age,
and Eris, fierce-hearted Strife.
