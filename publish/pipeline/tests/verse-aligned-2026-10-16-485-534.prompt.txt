EARLIER ENGLISH DRAFT
p034 (485–506): Rhea wrapped a great stone in baby clothes and handed it to the son of Ouranos, the mighty lord who then ruled the gods. He took it in his hands and swallowed it, the ruthless creature. He never realized that his own son had been left behind in the stone’s place, unconquerable and untroubled. Soon that son would overpower him with his own strong hands, drive him from his royal position, and rule among the immortals himself.

The young lord’s strength and splendid limbs grew quickly. As the years passed, great Cronus, with his crooked schemes, was tricked by Gaia’s deeply knowing advice and brought his children back up. First he vomited out the stone he had swallowed last. Zeus set it firmly in the broad earth at holy Pytho, in the valleys beneath Parnassus, to stand as a sign for future ages, a wonder for mortal people.

Zeus also freed his father’s brothers, the sons of Ouranos, from their terrible chains. Their father had recklessly bound them. They remembered his kindness and repaid it: they gave him thunder, the smoking thunderbolt, and lightning. Until then, vast Gaia had kept these hidden. Trusting in these weapons, Zeus rules over mortals and immortals.

p035 (507–520): Iapetus married Clymene, a daughter of Oceanus with lovely ankles, and shared her bed. She bore him Atlas, strong in spirit, and Menoetius, full of overbearing pride; Prometheus, clever and endlessly inventive; and Epimetheus, whose thinking went astray. From the start, Epimetheus brought trouble to people who live on bread: he was the first to welcome the virgin girl Zeus had made.

Zeus, whose gaze reaches far, struck the violent Menoetius with a smoking thunderbolt and sent him down into Erebus because of his reckless behavior and overpowering arrogance.

Atlas stands at the ends of the earth, in front of the clear-voiced Hesperides. Under harsh compulsion, he holds up the broad sky with his head and tireless hands. This was the fate Zeus, the wise planner, assigned him.

p036 (521–534): Zeus bound Prometheus, with all his intricate schemes, in painful chains he could not escape, driving a pillar through his middle. He sent a long-winged eagle against him, and it ate his immortal liver. Every night the liver grew back completely—as much as the wide-winged bird had eaten through the whole day.

Heracles, the mighty son of Alcmene with her lovely ankles, killed the eagle. He drove that cruel affliction away from Prometheus, son of Iapetus, and freed him from his suffering. This had the consent of Zeus, who rules on high from Olympus: he wanted the fame of Heracles, born in Thebes, to grow still greater across the earth that feeds so many.

Out of regard for this, Zeus honored his glorious son. Angry though Zeus was, he let go of the anger he had long held against Prometheus for matching his wits against the mighty son of Cronus.
