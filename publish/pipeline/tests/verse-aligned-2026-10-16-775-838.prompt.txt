p051 (775–806): There lives a goddess hated by the immortals:
dread Styx, eldest daughter of Oceanus,
the river that flows back on itself.
Far from the other gods she lives in a splendid house,
with towering rocks above it and silver pillars
supporting it on every side, reaching toward the sky.

Rarely does swift-footed Iris, daughter of Thaumas,
come across the sea’s broad back with a message.
But whenever quarrels and strife break out among the immortals,
and one of the gods who live on Olympus tells a lie,
Zeus sends Iris far away to bring back, in a golden jug,
the famous cold water by which the gods swear their great oath.
It trickles down from a high, sheer rock.

Far beneath the earth with its broad roads,
a branch of the sacred river Oceanus
flows through black night.
One tenth of his water has been set apart:
nine parts wind around the earth and the sea’s broad back
in silver swirls, then pour into the sea.
The one remaining part flows from the rock—
a great source of suffering for the gods.

If any immortal who lives on snowy Olympus
pours out this water and swears a false oath over it,
that god lies breathless for a full year.
They never come near ambrosia or nectar to eat or drink,
but lie without breath or speech on a bed spread for them,
wrapped in a dreadful coma.

When that sickness has lasted a full year,
another, harsher ordeal follows.
For nine years the god is cut off from the gods who live forever,
never joining their councils or their feasts
for all those nine years.
In the tenth, they join once more
the gatherings of the immortals who live on Olympus.

Such is the oath the gods established:
the ancient, unfailing water of Styx,
which flows through a harsh and rocky place.

p052 (807–819): There, side by side, are the sources and the limits
of dark earth, misty Tartarus,
the barren sea and the starry sky:
grim, moldy places that even the gods hate.

There stand marble gates and a bronze threshold,
unshakable, fixed on roots that stretch unbroken below,
grown there of itself.
Beyond the gates, apart from all the other gods,
the Titans live on the far side of gloomy Chaos.

But Cottus and Gyes, the famous allies
of loudly thundering Zeus,
live in homes at the foundations of Oceanus.
As for noble Briareus, the Earth-shaker Poseidon,
whose crashes thunder deep,
made him his son-in-law:
he gave him his daughter Cymopolea to marry.

p053 (820–838): After Zeus had driven the Titans out of heaven,
vast Gaia gave birth to her youngest child, Typhoeus,
joining in love with Tartarus through golden Aphrodite.

This powerful god had hands strong enough for mighty deeds,
and feet that never tired.
From his shoulders grew a hundred snake heads,
the heads of a terrifying serpent,
flicking their dark tongues.
Fire flashed beneath the brows
of the eyes in those monstrous heads;
from every head fire blazed as he stared.

Voices sounded in all his fearsome heads,
sending out every kind of incredible cry.
Sometimes they spoke so that the gods could understand;
sometimes they sounded like a proud, bellowing bull
whose force could not be held back;
sometimes like a lion with a ruthless heart;
sometimes like puppies—a wonder to hear.
At other times he hissed,
and the tall mountains echoed.

That day something beyond all help would have happened:
Typhoeus would have ruled over mortals and immortals,
if Zeus, the father of gods and humans,
had not been quick to notice.
