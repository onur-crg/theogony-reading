p037 (535–557): When gods and mortal humans were settling matters at Mecone, Prometheus eagerly divided a great ox and set the portions before them, trying to trick Zeus. In one portion he laid the meat and the rich, fatty organs inside the hide, covering them with the ox’s stomach. In the other he carefully arranged the ox’s white bones, hiding them under shining fat—a cunning trick.

Then the father of gods and humans said to him:
“Son of Iapetus, most glorious of all lords, my friend, how unevenly you have divided the portions!”

So Zeus mocked him, Zeus whose plans never fail. Prometheus, with his twisting schemes, answered with a quiet smile, still intent on his trick:
“Zeus, greatest and most glorious of the everlasting gods, choose whichever portion your heart tells you to.”

He spoke deceitfully. But Zeus, whose plans never fail, recognized the trick and saw it clearly. In his heart he foresaw troubles for mortal humans—troubles that would indeed come true.

With both hands he lifted the white fat. Anger filled his mind and heart when he saw the ox’s white bones laid out in that cunning trick.

Ever since then, the peoples of the earth have burned white bones for the immortal gods on altars fragrant with sacrifice.

p038 (558–569): Deeply angered, Zeus the cloud-gatherer said to him:
“Son of Iapetus, clever beyond all others, my friend, you still have not forgotten your cunning tricks!”

So spoke Zeus in anger, Zeus whose plans never fail. From then on, always remembering the trick, he would not give mortal people living on earth the tireless power of fire in ash wood.

But the noble son of Iapetus deceived him. Prometheus stole the far-shining glow of tireless fire in a hollow fennel stalk. It bit deep into the heart of Zeus, who thunders on high. His heart blazed with anger when he saw fire’s far-shining glow among humans.

p039 (570–589): At once, to repay humans for the fire, Zeus made an evil for them. Following the plan of Zeus, son of Cronus, famous Hephaestus, lame in both legs, shaped earth into the likeness of a modest young woman.

Bright-eyed Athena dressed and belted her in a silvery robe. With her own hands she lowered an intricately worked veil over her head, a wonder to see. Around her head she placed a golden crown, which famous Hephaestus himself had skillfully made with his hands to please his father Zeus.

The crown held many intricate figures, a wonder to see: creatures of the many kinds the land and sea support. He set many of these upon it, marvelous figures shining with beauty, like living creatures with voices of their own.

When he had made this beautiful evil in exchange for the good gift of fire, Hephaestus led her out to where the other gods and humans were. She stood proud in the finery given her by bright-eyed Athena, daughter of mighty Zeus.

Wonder seized the immortal gods and mortal humans when they saw this dangerous trap, which humans had no means to escape.
