p054 (839–868): Zeus thundered with a harsh and mighty crash. All around, the earth rang terribly, and so did the wide sky above, the sea, the streams of Oceanus, and Tartarus beneath the earth. Great Olympus shook beneath his immortal feet as the king sprang into action. The earth groaned beneath him.

Heat from both fighters gripped the violet-dark sea—from thunder and lightning, from the monster’s fire, from fiery blasts of wind and the blazing thunderbolt. All the earth boiled, and the sky and sea. Long waves raged against the shores on every side under the force of the immortal fighters, and the shaking would not stop. Hades, ruler of the dead below, trembled. So did the Titans deep in Tartarus around Cronus, at the endless roar and the terrible fighting.

Then Zeus gathered all his strength and took up his weapons: thunder, lightning, and the smoking thunderbolt. He leaped from Olympus and struck, scorching every monstrous head of the terrifying creature. When Zeus had beaten him down with blow after blow, Typhoeus collapsed, his limbs powerless, and the vast earth groaned.

Flames shot from the thunderstruck lord Typhoeus in the mountain’s dark and rugged valleys. A vast stretch of earth burned in the tremendous heat and melted like tin, heated by skilled young men in crucibles pierced with holes. Even iron, the strongest metal, yields to blazing fire in mountain valleys and melts in the sacred earth beneath Hephaestus’s hands. So the earth melted in the glare of the burning fire.

In bitter fury, Zeus hurled Typhoeus into wide Tartarus.

p055 (869–885): From Typhoeus comes the force of the moisture-laden winds, except for Notus, Boreas, and sky-clearing Zephyrus. These three come from the gods and are a great help to mortals. But the others blow across the sea as reckless gusts. They fall upon the misty water and rage in vicious storms, bringing great suffering to mortals. Now one wind blows, now another: they scatter ships and kill sailors. People who meet them at sea have no defense against their violence.

These winds also sweep across the boundless, flowering earth. They ruin the lovely work of earth-born people, filling the land with dust and a dreadful uproar.

When the blessed gods had finished their struggle and settled their contest with the Titans by force over who would hold power and honor, Gaia advised them to urge far-seeing Olympian Zeus to be king and ruler of the immortals. Zeus then shared out their honors among them.

p056 (886–900): Zeus, king of the gods, made Metis his first wife. She knew more than any other god or mortal. But when she was about to give birth to the bright-eyed goddess Athena, Zeus deceived her with cunning, coaxing words and swallowed her into his belly. He did this on the advice of Gaia and starry Uranus.

They had advised him to do this so that no one else would take Zeus’s royal place among the everlasting gods. For Metis was destined to bear children of great wisdom: first a daughter, bright-eyed Tritogeneia, equal to her father in strength and wise judgment. After that, Metis would bear a son with a fierce and mighty spirit, who would become king of gods and humans.

But before that could happen, Zeus swallowed Metis into his belly, so that the goddess could advise him on good and evil.
