p040 (590–616): From that first woman comes the race of women—a great trouble living among mortal men, sharing their plenty but not their crushing poverty.

Just as bees in their sheltered hives feed drones, partners in wrongdoing—the bees hurry about all day until sunset, building white honeycombs, while the drones stay inside the covered hives and gather the harvest of others’ hard work into their own bellies—so Zeus, who thunders on high, made women a curse for mortal men, partners in work that brings pain.

And he gave men another evil in place of a blessing. A man may avoid marriage and women’s troublesome ways and choose never to marry, but then he reaches miserable old age with no one to care for him. He has enough to live on while he is alive, but when he dies, distant relatives divide his property. If a man’s fate is to marry, and he gets a good wife with sound sense, then good and bad keep competing throughout his life. But if he ends up with a wife of a wicked kind, he lives with unending sorrow in his chest, deep in his heart and spirit. There is no cure for that misery.

So no one can fool Zeus or get around his plans. Even kindly Prometheus, son of Iapetus, did not escape Zeus’s heavy anger. For all his knowledge, a mighty chain holds him fast by force.

p041 (617–628): When their father Uranus first grew angry with Briareus, Cottus, and Gyges, he bound them in mighty chains. He was struck with dread at their overwhelming strength, their appearance, and their size, and he forced them to live beneath the wide-pathed earth.

There they stayed in pain, beneath the ground at the farthest edge, at the limits of the great earth. For a very long time they suffered, with deep grief in their hearts.

But Zeus, son of Cronus, and the other immortal gods whom lovely-haired Rhea bore through her love with Cronus brought them back into the light, following Gaia’s advice. Gaia herself had explained everything fully: with their help, the gods would win victory and shining glory.

p042 (629–638): For a long time the Titan gods and the children of Cronus had fought each other in fierce battles, enduring heart-rending toil. The proud Titans fought from high Mount Othrys. The other gods, the givers of good things, fought from Olympus—the gods whom lovely-haired Rhea bore after sharing Cronus’s bed.

With heart-rending anger against each other, they had fought without a break for ten full years. Neither side could find a way to end the bitter struggle. The war hung in an even balance.

p043 (639–653): But when Zeus had given the three brothers everything they needed—nectar and ambrosia, which the gods themselves eat—proud courage grew in all their chests. Once they had tasted the nectar and delicious ambrosia, Zeus, father of gods and humans, spoke to them:

“Listen to me, glorious children of Gaia and Uranus, so I can tell you what the heart in my chest urges me to say. For a very long time now, day after day, we have fought against each other for victory and power—the Titan gods and all of us born to Cronus.

“Now show your great strength and your irresistible hands against the Titans in this grim battle. Remember the kindness we showed you: after all your suffering, our plans brought you back into the light, out of those cruel chains and the misty darkness.”
